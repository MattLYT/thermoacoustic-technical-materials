## Abstract

The classical thermoacoustic refrigeration adopts gases with ideal-gas properties as working fluids. Herein, the thermoacoustic refrigeration using a compressible liquid as the working fluid is investigated for the first time. Two models are utilized parallelly: a highly-simplified short refrigerator model and a far-more realistic full-scale thermoacoustic refrigerator model. The results from the short refrigerator model show that, ideally, the coefficient of performance can reach above 70% of the Carnot limit. In the full-scale system discussion, the coefficient of performance of the system can reach as high as 4.1 in a typical working condition for air conditioning (pumping heat from 285 K to 310 K), with respect to 36% of the Carnot limit. ## 1. Introduction

A thermoacoustic refrigerator is a device that consumes acoustic power to pump heat from a low-temperature heat source to a high-temperature environment through thermoacoustic effect. It generally has four main components: a stack/regenerator for thermoacoustic conversion, heat exchangers for heat transfer and creating a temperature gradient along the stack/regenerator, ducts to create proper acoustic field and a sound source to drive the system. Its extremely simple structure endows a thermoacoustic refrigerator with advantages of high reliability and low cost. Thus the thermoacoustic refrigerator has been used in space applications (Garrett et al., 1993), household refrigeration (Chi et al., 2021, Chi et al., 2022), electronic device cooling (Chen and Xu, 2022) and natural gas liquefaction (Wang et al., 2022, Wang et al., 2023). Generally, gases (helium, nitrogen and argon, etc.) with ideal-gas behaviors are adopted in thermoacoustic refrigerators. However, conventional thermoacoustic refrigerators still suffer from problems like low energy density (Chen et al., 2021).

Limited by daily experience, people often have a false idea that liquids cannot be compressed and thus is thermodynamically inert. Consequently, a liquid has rarely been considered as the working fluid for heat engines. However, a liquid may become a very good working fluid under specific conditions. Let us first consider $T \beta$ , which is an important parameter to estimate whether a fluid is proper for thermoacoustic conversion because it affects the conversion directly (the acoustic source term is proportional to it). For a fluid in the range of $0 . 7 T _ { c r } \lesssim T \lesssim T _ { c r }$ and $p > p _ { c r }$ , the value of $T \beta$ can be close to or even beyond unity, which is the value for ideal gases, otherwise $T \beta$ may be too low to be used for thermoacoustic systems. Therefore, to ensure the thermodynamic activity, a liquid is only suitable for low temperature difference applications including waste heat recovery and air conditioning (Swift, 1989b). Secondly, heat capacity $\rho c _ { p }$ of liquids is much larger than that of gases, leading to a much higher heat transfer coefficient (Allen et al., 1980). Lastly, the characteristic acoustic impedance $\rho _ { m } a$ of liquids is orders of magnitude higher than that of gases, which helps the parcels to oscillate with a large pressure amplitude but a small velocity amplitude. This significantly reduces the risk of explosion, and allows a high-power system with a very small stroke (Allen et al., 1981b).

The idea of using a liquid as the working fluid in heat engines was firstly proposed by J.F.J Malone about one hundred years ago (Malone, 1929, Malone, 1924). In the engines he proposed, the piston and the displacer move back and forth repeatedly, while the fluid inside the heat exchangers and the regenerator moves in a unidirectional pulsating way with the help of check valves. In one of his engines, liquid water with pressure as high as 700 bar was used as the working fluid. A thermal efficiency of 20% was achieved, with a brake power up to 368 kW (500 horse power) (Malone, 1931). This performance was superior to steam engines for powering ships and locomotives during that era. In fact, Malone was confident with the commercial application of his engine, but he could not realize this due to the Great Depression and the rapid development of other engines (internal combustion engine, for instance).

The above-mentioned engine was called Malone engine, which had been unfortunately forgotten for a long time, until a group from the University of California led by Wheatley (Allen et al., 1981a, Allen et al., 1980, Allen et al., 1981b) rediscovered this idea in 1980s. They realized that the nature of Malone engine is a Stirling-cycle engine but with a liquid as the working fluid, although with characteristics of Brayton cycle. Later, Swift (1989b) developed a Stirling system with liquid propylene under a mean pressure of 102 bar as the working fluid. It can be considered as four alpha-type Stirling engines connected in series by a common crankshaft, with a phase difference of 90° between adjacent sub-engines. To reduce the viscous loss, the regenerator was multi-layer stacked metal sheets composed of spiraled 0.15- mm-thick stainless-steel films, with a gap of 40 μm between adjacent layers. The gap was controlled by the bumps on the

sheet (Swift et al., 1983). The system could operate as either an engine or a refrigerator. In the refrigerator mode, it could obtain a coefficient of performance of 1.8 (corresponding to 20% of the Carnot limit) when pumping heat with a temperature difference of around 30 K to the ambient temperature environment (Swift 1989b, Swift, 1989a).

Given the essential similarity between thermoacoustic and Stirling systems (Ceperley, 1979), a liquid thermoacoustic engine has also been proposed by Migliori and Swift (1988). Liquid sodium was used as the working fluid. The engine produced 18 W acoustic power with a thermal efficiency of 1.8%. Moreover, liquid $\mathrm { C O } _ { 2 }$ was also suggested as the working fluid (Swift and Brown, 1994), but no further study was carried out. Recently, a research group led by Scalo in Purdue University reported a series of theoretical and experimental studies on thermoacoustic engines using fluids near critical points, observing significantly reduced temperature difference to drive the engine and a very high pressure amplitude (Migliorino and Scalo, 2020, Scalo et al., 2017, Martinez et al., 2021a, Martinez et al., 2021b, Alexander et al., 2018). However, their works are on engines with trans-critical fluids.

Motivated by the above mentioned studies, the compressible liquid might be a promising working fluid for thermoacoustic refrigeration with its unique advantages. However, up until now,the liquid thermoacoustic refrigeration has yet to be studied in details, and its mechanism and potential in performance remain unclear. Herein, a systematic theoretical study on the liquid thermoacoustic refrigeration, especially in the travelingwave acoustic field, is presented. Both a highly-simplified short refrigerator model and a far-more realistic full-scale thermoacoustic refrigerator model are used. Our results demonstrate that the liquid thermoacoustic refrigeration can be efficient, towards the conventional gas thermoacoustic refrigeration in low-temperature lift applications, and with a more compact configuration.

## 2.2. Model system

## 2.2.1. Short refrigerator

We first propose a short refrigerator model. As shown in Fig. 1(a), the short refrigerator is actually a short parallel-plate type regenerator, which is the only place where thermoacoustic conversion happens in a traveling-wave thermoacoustic system, with the length of ∆x. It is so short that the values of $p _ { 1 } , U _ { 1 } , T _ { m }$

and the properties of the fluid inside it can be treated constant. Then the influence of the liquid on the performance can be decoupled from the acoustic field and the geometry of the system. Therefore, this idealized model can be used to investigate the full potential of a specific thermoacoustic cycle, regardless of the geometry of the other components in a full-scale system (Swift and Fusco, 1988, Yang et al., 2022a). Additionally, this short structure is called a refrigerator, because it pumps heat from its lower-temperature end to its higher-temperature end, with the consumption of acoustic power. This actually follows the idea of the “short engine" in Swift (1988).

The acoustic power consumed by the short refrigerator can be calculated by

$$
\begin{array} { r } { \dot { \varDelta t _ { 2 } } = - \frac { \varDelta x } { 2 } \Re p _ { 1 } \frac { \tilde { d U _ { 1 } } } { d x } + \frac { d p _ { 1 } } { d x } \tilde { U _ { 1 } } } \end{array}\tag{7}
$$

## 2.2.2. Full-scale traveling-wave refrigerator

To give a more practical estimation of the performance of the liquid thermoacoustic refrigeration, we also propose a four-stage traveling-wave thermoacoustic refrigerator. As can be seen in Fig. 1(b), the system has four identical stages, connected in series with a phase delay of $9 0 ^ { \circ }$ . In each stage, there is a regenerator sandwiched by an ambient heat exchanger (AHX) and a cold heat exchanger (CHX). A second AHX separates the tube connecting two stages into two sections, one is the thermal buffer tube (TBT) with a temperature gradient along it, the other one is the resonator which has no temperature gradient. This design is chosen because it is a mature design for classical thermoacoustic systems, able to provide a stable acoustic field dominated by traveling-wave components (Blok, 2012, Zhang et al., 2016). The system was designed with the thermoacoustic generator developed by Bi et al. (2017) as the reference. The details of each component can be seen in Table 1. As for the hydraulic radius of the regenerator, it varies around 10 μm, to satisfy a specific ωτ. Additionally, the acoustic source can be either a linear motor or a loudspeaker, or any other devices that can provide desired acoustic oscillations. It is treated as a black box. The frequency is set to be 60 Hz, with considerations of a proper frequency for the operation of linear motors (assume that they are used as acoustic sources) (Timmer et al., 2018) and the hydraulic radius to be not too small. At the joint of the acoustic source and the trunk resonator, $U _ { 1 }$ is discontinuity and the Kirchhoff's law is obeyed, i.e., $U _ { 1 , o u t } = U _ { 1 , i n } + U _ { 1 , a s }$ (see Fig. 1(b)), while $p _ { 1 }$ stays unchanged.

## 3. Results and discussion

## 3.1. Properties of liquid

The uniqueness of the liquid thermoacoustic refrigeration comes from fluid properties. We exemplify this with propylene in Fig. 3. As can be seen, $\beta$ goes to divergence at the critical point. There is also a peak for each curve when $p _ { m } > p _ { c r } ,$ but its value decreases as $p _ { m }$ is increased. Obviously, in a wide temperature range $\left( T _ { m } \right)$ 300 K) when $p _ { m } > p _ { c r }$ which covers the requirement of air conditioning and some industrial heat pumps, $\beta$ of propylene can be comparable to or even larger than that of the ideal gas. It implies that a liquid can be thermodynamically powerful for thermoacoustic conversion, lying the foundation of an efficient liquid thermoacoustic refrigerator. $\beta$ gradually decreases when $T _ { m } < 2 5 0 ~ \mathrm { K }$ , because the fluid is approaching towards incompressible, which is not suitable as a working fluid. A smaller $P r$ is also important, which means a lower viscous loss relative to the thermal effect (Tijani et al., 2002). The trends of $P r$ are similar to those of $\beta .$ However, a liquid always has $P r > 1$

while $P r < 1$ for most gases. This is an obstacle when adopting a liquid. Additionally, Pr goes to very high near critical point, indicating severe viscous loss. The trends of $\rho$ is also shown. There is a sudden decrease near critical point when $T _ { m }$ is lowered.

To illustrate a more straightforward understanding on how the unique properties affect thermoacoustic conversion, we reconsider the thermoacoustic conversion in the regenerator (or the short refrigerator). By rearranging Eqs. (1), (2), the variation of acoustic power along the axial direction can be expressed as (Swift, 2002)

$$
\begin{array} { r } { \frac { \dot { d E } _ { 2 } } { d x } =  - \frac { r _ { \nu } } { 2 } | U _ { 1 } | ^ { 2 } - \frac { 1 } { 2 r _ { \kappa } } \Big | p _ { 1 } \Big | ^ { 2 } + \frac { 1 } { 2 } \Re { g \tilde { p } _ { 1 } } U _ { 1 } } \end{array}\tag{15}
$$

where the three terms represent the viscous loss, the thermalrelaxation loss and the acoustic power consumed by thermoacoustic conversion. Hence, $r _ { \nu }$ and $r _ { \kappa }$ can be considered the acoustic resistances due to the viscous and the thermalrelaxation effects, and $g$ is the acoustic source term which directly shows the effect of thermoacoustic conversion. Their expressions are

$$
\begin{array} { r } { r _ { \nu } = \frac { \omega \rho _ { m } \hspace \Im - f _ { \nu } } { A _ { g a s } { \lvert 1 - f _ { \nu } \rvert } ^ { 2 } } } \end{array}\tag{16}

The heat transfer between the working fluid and the solid wall in heat exchangers is critical in thermoacoustic refrigerators. ## 3.2. Short refrigerator

According to Eq. (11), since the fluid is propylene and the phase difference is $\varphi = 0$ , there are five parameters important: $T _ { m } , \nabla T _ { m } / \omega _ { \mathrm { \scriptscriptstyle m } }$ |z|, ωτ and $p _ { m }$ Their influences on the performance of the short refrigerator will be discussed in this section.

In Fig. 5, the values of $C O P _ { R }$ with the variation of ωτ, under different $\nabla T _ { m } /$ ω or |z| are presented. $p _ { m }$ is fixed at 10 MPa. Similar to the thermoacoustic conversion with gas (Yazaki et al., 1998), Womersley number ωτ is a key parameter governing the thermoacoustic conversion with liquid, which is reflected in Eqs. (11), (6b). It represents the ratio between the hydraulic radius over the thermal penetration depth, reflecting a trade-off between the viscous loss and the thermal effect, because it can be rearranged as the ratio of the thermal time scale $\left( r _ { h } ^ { 2 } / 2 \kappa \right)$ to the oscillation time scale $\left( 1 / \omega \right)$ . If ωτ is too large, the thermoacoustic conversion only happens in part of the channel near the wall and the conversion is not efficient because the fluid in the center of the channel is wasted, although the viscous loss may be low; If ωτ is too small, the very thin channel will cause severe viscous loss and decrease efficiency (Yazaki et al., 1998). As a result, an optimal ωτ exists for highest $C O P _ { R }$ in each curve, as shown in Fig. 5(a). Furthermore, the peak $C O P _ { R }$ shows a decreasing trend as $\nabla T _ { m } /$ ω is increased. For lower values of $\nabla T _ { m } /$ ω, the dependence of $C O P _ { R }$ on ωτ is less sensitive, and the optimal range of ωτ for high $C O P _ { R }$ is larger. It helps the whole regenerator to be efficient in a full-scale system, where ωτ varies significantly along the regenerator due to the variation of temperature. This is in accordance with the descriptions in Swift (1989b), where it is stated that the liquid fluid only fits for low temperature difference applications.

From Fig. 5(b), it is clear that the acoustic impedance |z| strongly affects $C O P _ { R }$ |z| larger than 20 $\rho _ { m } a$ should be enough for efficient conversion, which is similar to the values suggested in gas traveling-wave thermoacoustic systems (Gardner and Swift, 2003). Otherwise, a sever viscous loss may occur, due to the large velocity amplitude relative to the pressure amplitude. The peak appears near $\omega \tau \sim 0$ . 1 in all curves, implying that the optimal hydraulic radius of the regenerator weakly depends on |z|. Additionally, the values of $C O P _ { R }$ can be above 70% if ωτ, $\nabla T _ { m } / \omega$ and |z| are properly set, indicating that the thermoacoustic refrigeration with the compressible liquid as the working fluid can be efficient.

The influences of temperature and mean pressure on the performance of the short refrigerator are shown in Fig. 6. Note that ωτ is optimized at every point, and $| z |$ is fixed at a relatively large value, so the results here can be considered the theoretical limit of $C O P _ { R }$ for a liquid thermoacoustic refrigerator working under similar conditions. The dependence of $C O P _ { R }$ on $p _ { m }$ is weak, unless near the critical point. On the other hand, the operating temperature $T _ { m }$ has a significant influence on the performance of the system. $C O P _ { R }$ increases with the rise of $T _ { m }$ until reaches a peak. The location of the peak in each curve is near that in Fig. 3, as a trade-off between the gains and the losses. However, at the critical point, $C O P _ { R }$ drops significantly towards zero. This tendency indicates that the thermoacoustic refrigeration stops working at the critical point, because of the sudden rise of viscous and thermal-relaxation losses, although $| g |$ is also very high (see Fig. 3). Therefore, a thermoacoustic refrigerator should work away from the critical point, and a mean pressure higher than $p _ { c r }$ is a proper choice. Additionally, $C O P _ { R }$ of ideal gas (helium) with $p _ { m } = 1 0$ MPa is also displayed. As can be seen, $C O P _ { R }$ of helium gradually decreases due to the increasing viscosity. Compared with liquid propylene, $C O P _ { R }$ of helium has a much weaker dependence on $T _ { m } ,$ because of the weaker dependence of ideal-gas properties. The comparison shows that the potential of efficiency with a compressible liquid may be lower than that with an ideal gas.

## 3.3. Full-scale system

In this section, the performance of the full-scale liquid thermoacoustic refrigerator will be discussed.

In Fig. 7, the values of $C O P _ { R } ^ { s }$ and $Q _ { c }$ of the system under different ωτ and various temperature difference $\varDelta T _ { m }$ are shown. Obviously, an optimal ωτ exists for the highest $C O P _ { R } ^ { s } .$ The optimal ωτ decreases from 0.37 to 0.2 when $\varDelta T _ { m }$ is increased from 10 K to 40 K. When $\varDelta T _ { m }$ is increased, $C O P _ { R } ^ { s }$ shows a weaker dependence on the variation of ωτ. These are in accordance with the conclusion from the short refrigerator analysis (see Fig. 5). For the air conditioning application where a typical $\varDelta T _ { m }$ is 25 K, ωτ in the range of 0.2 to 0.4 is suggested. The highest $C O P _ { R } ^ { s }$ is 36% with $\Delta T _ { m } = 2 5 \mathrm { K }$ (the corresponding $C O P ^ { s }$ is 4.1), which is lower than the peak values in the short refrigerator (see Fig. 5). The main reason behind this is that in the full-scale refrigerator, the losses in components including heat exchangers, the resonator and the thermal buffer tube are included, while they are not considered in the short refrigerator. If these losses are ignored, the corresponding $C O P _ { R } ^ { s }$ can be increased to 42%.

In Fig. 7(b), the corresponding cooling power $Q _ { c }$ is also presented. $Q _ { c }$ rises with the increase of $\omega \tau ,$ until reaches a peak at around 0.1 to 0.15, then decreases gradually as ωτ is further increased. $Q _ { c }$ increases as $\varDelta T _ { m }$ is decreased, because the acoustic power consumed to pump a specific amount of heat is reduced.

## 4. Conclusions

We have investigated the thermoacoustic refrigeration with compressible liquid as the working fluid based on two models. The first model is the highly-simplified short refrigerator, which can be used to investigate the full potential of the liquid thermoacoustic refrigeration without considering the specific configuration of the system. The second model is a full-scale acoustic-driven traveling-wave thermoacoustic refrigerator, which gives a more practical estimation on its performance.

In the short refrigerator discussion, the factors affecting the liquid thermoacoustic conversion, including temperature, the ratio of temperature gradient over angular frequency, acoustic impedance and Womersley number, have been examined. By properly setting these parameters, the coefficient of performance can reach as high as 70% of the Carnot limit. This indicates that the thermoacoustic refrigeration with a compressible liquid working fluid has the potential to be as efficient as the conventional gas system. At the critical point, the performance may be ruined by the large losses, so the operation should be far away from the critical point.

In the full-scale system discussion, it can be concluded the acoustical characteristics in a liquid system are similar to those in a gas system, so the basic designs for gas thermoacoustic refrigerators may be also applicable for a liquid thermoacoustic refrigerator. In a typical working condition for the air conditioning application (pumping heat from 285 K to 310 K), the coefficient of performance of the system can reach as high as 4.1, with respect to 36% of the Carnot limit. When pumping heat from 332 K to 357 K, the coefficient of performance can reach 44% of Carnot limit.

Its advantages over the gas system mostly lie on its much smaller displacement amplitude when delivering a given amount of acoustic power, which reduces the displacement amplitude of the motor's piton, and the effective length of the heat exchanger. As a result, both the linear motor and the heat exchanger can be much more compact, so that the mass and the volume of the system can be greatly reduced, as well as the cost. Its potential application is the low-temperature difference refrigeration and heat pumping (air conditioning, electronics cooling, etc.). However, to achieve the very high mean pressure for the proper fluid state, which is higher than that in most classical thermoacoustic systems, a charging system and a more stringent design on the pressure resistance are needed. This disadvantage is acceptable in a lab environment, but might be a hindrance for the further development of such systems.
