# A thermoacoustic combined cooling, heating, and power (CCHP) system for waste heat and LNG cold energy recovery

Jingyuan Xu<sup>a\*</sup>, Ercang Luo<sup>b,c</sup>, Simone Hochgreb<sup>a</sup>

a. Department of Engineering, University of Cambridge, Cambridge CB2 1PZ, United Kingdom

b. CAS Key Laboratory of Cryogenics, Technical Institute of Physics and Chemistry, Chinese Academy

of Sciences, Beijing 100190, China

c. University of Chinese Academy of Sciences, Beijing 100049, China

## Abstract

Waste energy recovery is crucial for reducing greenhouse gas emissions and alleviating the increasing demand for central power grids. In the paper, theoretical analyses have been carried out on a novel thermoacoustic combined cooling, heating, and power system for simultaneously recovering waste heat and liquefied natural gas cold energy. The system is designed based on acoustic impedance matching between the thermoacoustic and alternator units for maximizing the overall exergy efficiency. The efficiency, cost savings, and emission reductions of the optimal system are investigated. With cold and waste heat temperatures of 130 K and 500 K, the system achieves an overall exergy efficiency of 24.1%, allowing 78.4 MWh of primary fuel energy reduction, and correspondingly, 30.6 tons of $\mathrm { C O } _ { 2 }$ emissions reduction per year. Investigations are further performed on the system parametric sensitivities. A method for adjusting the alternator external electric impedance is proposed to allow for matching piston displacement within design parameters. Besides, comparisons are made with alternative thermoacoustic cogeneration models. The results show that the proposed system can operate effectively for various supply modes, which is promising for adaptation in different applications. Finally, comparisons are drawn with similar existing technologies and future developments are discussed.

## 1 Introduction

World energy demands are expected to dramatically increase by up to 28% from 2015 to 2040 [1] with growing populations and economies. Technologies that recover waste energy, i.e., waste heat and waste cold energy, provide the opportunity to improve energy utilization efficiency and reduce greenhouse gas emission. Over recent decades, the world has focused on recovering waste heat energy from renewable energy sources (solar and geothermal) [2,3] as well as industrial processes [4,5]. Current active technologies for utilizing waste heat include sorption systems [6], mechanically driven heat pumps [7] and organic Rankine cycles [8]. A number of studies have recently focused on waste cold energy recovery [9,10], given the potential of liquefied natural gas (LNG), since LNG releases around 830 kJ/kg of cold energy during the regasification process before combustion. Given the global annual LNG regasification capacity of 824 MTPA [11], the potential cold energy corresponds to $6 . 8 3 8 \times 1 0 ^ { 1 4 } \mathrm { k J }$ per year. However, such a substantial amount of available cold energy remains underutilized in the traditional regasification stage.

The combination of waste heat recovery and LNG available energy utilization can further improve energy utilization efficiency, given the higher potential availability between the two states. However, most current systems are based on the Rankine cycle, which is cost-effective only for energy utilization on a scale of megawatts. In addition to waste energy existing on a large scale, there is abundantly available waste energy on a much smaller scale in distributed energy systems. Therefore, the development of technically and economically viable energy solutions for recovering such small-scale waste energy remains a challenge.

Thermoacoustic technology may offer an alternative for harnessing such smallscale waste energy. This heat-driven technology can be applied for heating [12], cooling [13,14] or power generation [15,16], and works by converting thermal energy into acoustic power in the thermoacoustic engine and using acoustic power to pump heat via a thermoacoustic heat pump or to generate electricity via an alternator. Thermoacoustic technology involves no harmful ozone-depleting gases or mechanical moving parts and offers potential low-cost installation based on simple components. Thermoacoustic systems can effectively operate at power ranges from a few tens of watts to dozens of kilowatts. Thermoacoustic technology, therefore, represents a promising environmentally friendly, reliable, and simple way of recovering small-scale waste energy.

Thermoacoustic systems for recovering waste heat energy have been developed by many researchers. In 2010, de Blok developed a looped thermoacoustic refrigerator [17], and the system achieved a cooling power of 78.2 W and an exergy efficiency of 9.3% at heating and cooling temperatures of 442 K and 240 K, respectively. In 2012,

Yu et al. developed a prototype of a thermoacoustic electricity generator for the utilization of low-grade heat energy [18], and the experimental results depicted an onset temperature difference of about 120 K. In 2014, Mumith et al designed a thermoacoustic engine for waste heat recovery in food manufacturing [19]. The calculation results show the engine can deliver 1 kW of output acoustic power with an exergy efficiency of about 17%. In 2017, Wang et al. numerically studied a four-stage looped thermoacoustic Stirling power generator [20]. The simulation result depicted a maximum electric power of 1.2 kW and the highest exergy efficiency of around 20%. In 2018, Jin et al. conducted experimental studies of a looped three-stage thermoacoustic electric generator [21], and the demonstrator achieved an exergy efficiency of 5% at the heating temperature of 367 K. Later, Hamood et al. developed a prototype of a two-stage thermoacoustic electricity generator to recover waste heat from exhaust gases in internal combustion engines [22]. The demonstrator exhibited electric power of around 50 W with an exergy efficiency of 11% at a heating temperature of 590 K. In 2020, Yang et al. theoretically studied a traveling-wave phasechange thermoacoustic engine [23]. When using the engine to drive an alternator, an exergy efficiency of 27 % and electrical power of 10 W were expected to be achieved at a heating temperature of 340 K. Recently, Xu et al. proposed a thermoacoustic refrigerator with hybrid gas-liquid resonators for waste heat recovery [24]. At heating and cooling temperatures of 400 K and 270 K respectively, their calculation results demonstrated that a four-stage cooling system was able to provide a cooling power of 1.7 kW and an exergy efficiency of around 22 %.

In addition to using thermoacoustic systems for waste heat utilization, other efforts have been directed at recovering the available cold energy from LNG regasification processes. Wang et al. proposed a thermoacoustic Stirling generator for LNG cold and waste heat recovery. Their system numerically achieved an output electric power of 2.3 kW and an exergy efficiency of about 25 % at heating and cooling temperatures of 500 K and 110 K respectively [25]. Hou et al. numerically studied a thermoacoustic Stirling electrical generator for LNG cold energy recovery. With cooling and heating temperatures of 110 K and 303 K respectively, their system exhibited the highest exergy efficiency of about 21 % [26].

However, current investigations of thermoacoustic systems for waste energy recovery have been limited to single-generation applications (i.e., either for cooling, heating, or electricity), which may hinder their wider usage in a variety of applications. Another limitation of the current waste-recovery thermoacoustic system is the low power generation from either waste heat or available cold temperature gradients. In this paper, we proposed a first-ever concept of thermoacoustic system to tackle these two key issues: (1) multiple thermodynamic cycles are integrated in one system to enable different energy-conversion processes, and hence resulting in combined provisions of heating, cooling and electricity; (2) combined utilizations of waste heat and cold energy allows for the larger power amplification factor relatively to single-temperature gradient thermoacoustic systems (demonstrated in Section 3), leading to significantly higher power per unit volume of heat exchanger. To the best of the authors’ knowledge, no research has been done on combining a thermoacoustic multi-generation system with the utilization of both waste heat and LNG cold energy.

This paper aims to design a thermoacoustic multi-generation system for waste heat and LNG cold energy recovery based on a maximum exergy-based objective, and to present the working characteristics of the system in detail. In Section 2, system configuration and performance indicators are introduced. In Section 3, the performance of the thermoacoustic engine units when driven by three types of energy sources (heat, cold energy, and combined heat and cold) are discussed. Section 4 presents the design method of the thermoacoustic trigeneration system. Section 5 shows the working characteristics of the thermoacoustic trigeneration system, including system performance, effects of key operation conditions, and parameter sensitivity control. In Section 6, further investigations are conducted to explore thermoacoustic cogeneration systems, and comparisons are made to the thermoacoustic trigeneration system. In Section 7, the proposed thermoacoustic multi-generation systems are further compared to the existing technologies and their future potential applications are introduced. Section 8 presents conclusions.

## 2. System description

## 2.1 Configuration

Figure 1(a) shows a schematic of the four-stage thermoacoustic trigeneration system. The system incorporates four identical energy-conversion subunits, as shown in Figure 1(b), in a loop through resonance tubes. In each subunit, a thermoacoustic engine unit is directly connected to a thermoacoustic heat pump, between which a double-sided linear alternator is coupled as a branch. The thermoacoustic engine unit consists of an ambient-temperature heat exchanger, a thermal buffer tube, a lowtemperature heat exchanger, a regenerator, and a high-temperature heat exchanger. The engine unit converts thermal energy into acoustic power when a temperature gradient is applied along the regenerator. Waste heat flows through the high-temperature heat exchanger, and LNG flows through the low-temperature heat exchanger. The heat pump unit consists of a high-temperature heat exchanger, a regenerator, and a lowtemperature heat exchanger; the heat pump unit uses the acoustic power generated to pump heat from the cold to the ambient side, thus generating a temperature gradient along with the regenerator. The alternator consists of two identical resonant linear motor units in a balanced opposed arrangement with clearance seal pistons, which compress a gas in a common front chamber and are all enclosed in a bolted pressure vessel (further details to be shown in Figure 6). The coils of the linear alternators are connected in series with a variable electric impedance, thereby dissipating the generated electricity.

The working principles of the overall system are as follows: when the axial temperature gradient across the regenerator of the engine unit exceeds a critical value, a self-excited thermoacoustic oscillation is generated and acoustic power is amplified. Part of the amplified acoustic power is used in the alternator unit to generate electric power. The remaining acoustic power is consumed by the heat pump unit which pumps heat from the low-temperature to high-temperature exchangers, where cooling and heating power are exchanged, respectively. The remaining acoustic power at the outlet of the heat pump is transmitted by a resonance tube to the next subunit. Finally, acoustic power is recycled into the next subunit.

## 2.2 Performance indicators

Both energy and exergy efficiencies are of interest in understanding system performance. The energy efficiency is evaluated as useful cooling, heating, and work output per unit heat input of the cycle:

$$
\eta _ { e n , o w e } = \frac { Q _ { c } + Q _ { h } + W _ { e } } { Q _ { w } }\tag{1}
$$

where $Q _ { w } , Q _ { h } ,$ and $\mathcal { Q } _ { c }$ are the waste heat thermal energy, pumped heat and cooling power, respectively, and $W _ { e }$ is the output electric power.

The energy efficiency is often insufficient to evaluate system performance in that it cannot quantify the performance of a system compared to a reversible cycle. Further, energy efficiency only takes into account the quantity of the thermodynamic losses rather than the quality of the energy. The exergy efficiency is used to assess the effectiveness of energy resource utilization in a thermal system. In the present case, the exergy efficiency of the overall system, $\eta _ { \mathrm { { e x , o v e } } } ,$ is the ratio of the sum of the exergy flow output from the CCHP system to the exergy flow-rate in the heat and cold supply stream:

$$
\eta _ { e x , o v e } = \frac { Q _ { c } ( \frac { T _ { 0 } } { T _ { c } } - 1 ) + Q _ { h } ( 1 - \displaystyle \frac { T _ { 0 } } { T _ { h } } ) + W _ { e } } { Q _ { L } ( \displaystyle \frac { T _ { 0 } } { T _ { L } } - 1 ) + Q _ { w } ( 1 - \displaystyle \frac { T _ { 0 } } { T _ { w } } ) }\tag{2}
$$

where $\mathcal { Q } _ { L }$ is the supplied LNG cold energy. $T _ { 0 } , \ T _ { c } , \ T _ { h } , \ T _ { L }$ , and $T _ { w }$ are the ambient temperature, cooling temperature, heating temperature, LNG temperature and waste heat temperature, respectively.

In addition to energy and exergy performance, the fuel energy saving (FES) is also of interest, as the total energy saved in the proposed CCHP system in comparison with separate dedicated conventional energy systems consisting of a boiler, electrical chiller, and conventional power stations. The annual fuel energy saving can be written as:

$$
F E S = ( \frac { W _ { e } } { \eta _ { c o n } } + \frac { Q _ { h } } { \eta _ { b o i } } + \frac { Q _ { c } } { C O P _ { c } } ) \tau _ { a n u }\tag{3}
$$

where $\eta _ { \mathrm { c o n } } , \eta _ { \mathrm { b o i } }$ and $C O P _ { \mathrm { c } }$ are the efficiency of the conventional power plant, efficiency of the boiler, and coefficient of performance of the electrical chiller, respectively. $\eta _ { \mathrm { c o n } } ,$ $\eta _ { \mathrm { b o i } }$ and $C O P _ { \mathrm { c } }$ for the conventional energy systems are taken here to be 40% [27], 82% [28] and 2.37 [29] respectively. The annual operating time, $\tau _ { \mathrm { a n u } } ,$ is 7200 hours.

The annual energy cost saving of the proposed system, $C _ { \mathrm { s } } ,$ can be estimated by:

$$
C _ { s } = ( \frac { W _ { e } } { \eta _ { c o n } } c _ { e l } + \frac { Q _ { h } } { \eta _ { b o i } } c _ { n g } + \frac { Q _ { c } } { C O P _ { c } } c _ { e l } ) \tau _ { a n u }\tag{4}
$$

where $c _ { \mathrm { e l } }$ is the price of electricity, 0.174 €/kWh in the $\mathrm { U K } ; c _ { \mathrm { n g } }$ is the price of natural gas, 0.058 €/kWh in the UK [30].

The potential $\mathrm { C O } _ { 2 }$ emission reduction (ER) has been the main driving force for the increasing interest in the market growth of waste energy utilization technologies. The annual reduction of $\mathrm { C O } _ { 2 }$ emission by using the proposed system can be estimated as:

$$
E R = ( \frac { W _ { e } } { \eta _ { c o n } } f _ { e l } + \frac { Q _ { h } } { \eta _ { b o i } } f _ { n g } + \frac { Q _ { c } } { C O P _ { c } } f _ { e l } ) \tau _ { a n u }\tag{5}
$$

where $f _ { \mathrm { e l } }$ is the $\mathrm { C O } _ { 2 }$ emission factor of natural gas, 0.5246 kgCO<sub>2</sub>/kWh; $f _ { \mathrm { n g } }$ is the $\mathrm { C O } _ { 2 }$ emission factor of natural gas, 0.1836 kgCO<sub>2</sub>/kWh [30].

## 4. Design of the thermoacoustic trigeneration system

Section 3 shows that the thermoacoustic engine driven by the combined heat and cold energy achieves a much higher power amplification factor than a similar system driven by a single energy source. Following this encouraging result, this section further presents the design and optimization of the thermoacoustic trigeneration system driven by the combined waste heat and LNG cold energy. Section 4.1 introduces the design method, followed by the detailed analyses of the output characteristics of the thermoacoustic unit and the coupling of the alternator in Section 4.2 and 4.3. In Section 4.4, based on the designed alternator, further investigations are carried out on the effect of the external electric impedance to give a better understanding of the alternator characteristics.

## 4.1 Design method

In the thermoacoustic CCHP system, the thermoacoustic unit (which consists of an engine, a heat pump, and a resonance tube) largely depends on the acoustic impedance of the alternators, which acts as the acoustic load to the thermoacoustic unit.

In addition, the required acoustic impedance for coupling the thermoacoustic unit also affects alternator efficiency. Therefore, the acoustic impedance coupling between the thermoacoustic unit and the alternator is crucial to the overall performance. In such a strongly coupled system, the joint optimization of the alternator with the thermoacoustic unit as a whole system means that each unit may not work at its respective optimal performance. Besides, due to a large number of control parameters, it is challenging to realize the optimal impedance match between the thermoacoustic unit and alternator if a complete model of the whole system is used. An effective way of solving this problem is to introduce a load acoustic impedance to decouple the thermoacoustic and alternator units. Given the objective of maximizing the overall exergy efficiency, firstly it is required to find the optimal load acoustic impedance for the thermoacoustic unit to ensure the highest efficiency ofthe thermoacoustic unit; then the alternator should be designed to achieve the required load acoustic impedance as well as the maximum alternator efficiency simultaneously. In this way, they can be both operated at the optimal state, so the coupled system is able to achieve the highest efficiency at its full potential.

The design procedure for the present work is illustrated in Figure 4. The characteristics of the required load acoustic impedances $Z _ { \mathrm { a } }$ for the thermoacoustic unit are firstly analyzed independently; the optimal decision variable $Z _ { \mathrm { a } }$ is obtained when maximizing the thermoaocustic unit efficiency $\eta _ { \mathrm { t h e } } .$ . Second, the relationship between the alternator efficiency and the load acoustic impedances is analysed; the optimal $Z _ { \mathrm { a } }$ is taken into the above relationship, and the alternator piston diameter $D _ { \mathfrak { p } }$ and moving mass M are optimized to achieve the highest efficiency of the alternator $\eta _ { \mathrm { a l t } }$ . Finally, the thermoacoustic unit and the alternator are optimally coupled in terms of highest overall efficiency when they can both reach their respective highest efficiency. ## 4.2 Output characteristics of the thermoacoustic unit

Analogous to electric impedance, acoustic impedance is a convenient property for characterizing effects that occur when the sound wave meets the boundary between two phases. Here, a load acoustic impedance, defined as the ratio of pressure wave $P _ { 1 }$ to volume flow rate $U _ { 1 }$ , is used to replace the alternator. i.e.

$$
Z _ { a } \mathrm { = } \frac { P _ { 1 } } { U _ { 1 } } \mathrm { = } R _ { a } + i X _ { a } \mathrm { = } \frac { \left| P _ { 1 } \right| } { \left| U _ { 1 } \right| } \mathrm { c o s } \theta _ { p - U } + i \frac { \left| P _ { 1 } \right| } { \left| U _ { 1 } \right| } \mathrm { s i n } \theta _ { p - U }\tag{8}
$$

where $R _ { a }$ and $X _ { a }$ are the real and imaginary parts of acoustic impedance, respectively; | | is the amplitude of the complex number, and $\theta _ { p - U }$ is the phase difference between the pressure and volume flow rate waves.

## 4.3 Coupling of the alternators

The relationship among the alternator efficiency, the alternator parameters and the load acoustic impedance is analysed. Figure 6 shows the key parameters of the alternator. The governing equations for an alternator are as follows [32],

$$
\frac { \tau } { A _ { p } } U _ { 1 } { = } ( R _ { e , i n } { + } R _ { e , o u t } + i X _ { e } ) I _ { 1 }\tag{10}
$$

$$
\frac { \tau } { A _ { p } } I _ { 1 } + \frac { R _ { m } + i ( \omega M - K / \omega ) } { { A _ { p } } ^ { 2 } } U _ { 1 } = P _ { 1 }\tag{11}
$$

where $P _ { 1 }$ is the pressure wave at the inlet of the alternator, $I _ { 1 }$ is the electrical current of the alternator, $U _ { 1 }$ is the volume flow rate associated with the alternator’s motion, $A _ { p }$ is the area of the piston, $R _ { m }$ is the mechanical damping, τ is the transduction coefficient between acoustic power and electric power, and $X _ { e }$ is the electrical reactance.

![[mu_6912a831_659a93f37a5007ed1ccf.jpg]]  
Figure 6. Elements of the alternator

The total spring stiffness K consists of mechanical spring stiffness $K _ { m }$ and gas spring stiffness $K _ { g }$ that is obtained through

$$
K _ { g } = \frac { \gamma P _ { m } A _ { p } } { V _ { b } }\tag{12}
$$

where $\gamma$ is the specific heat ratio, $P _ { m }$ is the mean pressure, and $V _ { b }$ is the volume of the alternator back space. The acoustic impedance at the inlet of the alternator is

$$
R _ { a } + i X _ { a } = \frac { P _ { 1 } } { U _ { 1 } }\tag{13}
$$

By solving Eqs. (10), (11) and (13), the ratio $I _ { 1 } / U _ { 1 }$ can be expressed in terms of the acoustic impedance:

$$
\frac { I _ { 1 } } { U _ { 1 } } { = } \frac { { A _ { p } } ^ { 2 } R _ { a } - R _ { m } + i ( { A _ { p } } ^ { 2 } X _ { a } - ( \omega M - K / \omega ) ) } { A _ { p } \tau }\tag{14}
$$

The acoustic power input to the alternator, $W _ { a }$ , and the electric power produced by the alternator, $W _ { e } ,$ can be calculated respectively by

$$
W _ { a } = \frac { 1 } { 2 } \operatorname { R e } [ P { U _ { 1 } } ^ { * } ] = \frac { \tau } { 2 A _ { p } } \operatorname { R e } [ { U _ { 1 } } ^ { * } I _ { 1 } ] + \frac { R _ { m } } { 2 { A _ { p } } ^ { 2 } } { \left| U _ { 1 } \right| } ^ { 2 }\tag{15}
$$

$$
W _ { e } = \frac { 1 } { 2 } \mathrm { R e } [ ( I _ { 1 } R _ { e , o u t } ) I _ { 1 } ^ { * } ] = \frac { \tau } { 2 A _ { p } } \mathrm { R e } [ U _ { 1 } I _ { 1 } ^ { * } ] - \frac { 1 } { 2 } R _ { e , i n } \left| I _ { 1 } \right| ^ { 2 }\tag{16}
$$

where $\mathrm { R e } \left[ \right]$ refers to the real part of a complex number, and the superscript <sup>\*</sup> indicates complex conjugation. In Eq. (15), the term $( R _ { \mathrm { m } } / 2 A _ { \mathrm { p } } { } ^ { 2 } ) \vert U _ { 1 } \vert ^ { 2 }$ of the input acoustic power is consumed by the mechanical resistance, and the remainder of the input acoustic power, τRe $[ { U _ { 1 } } ^ { * } I _ { 1 } ] / ( 2 A _ { p } )$ , is converted to electric power. In Eq. (16), the electric power τRe $\smash { \{ U _ { 1 } / \ l _ { 1 } \} ^ { * } \rceil } / ( 2 A _ { p } )$ , is partly dissipated by the electric resistance via a portion of $R _ { \mathrm { e , i n } } | I _ { 1 } | ^ { 2 } / 2$ and the remainder is delivered as the output electric power $W _ { e }$ . Taking the ratio of Eqs. (15), (16) and substituting Eq. (14) into the equations gives the acoustic-to-electric efficiency of the alternator, $\eta _ { a l t } ,$

$$
\eta _ { a i t } = \frac { W _ { e } } { W _ { a } } = 1 - \frac { R _ { m } } { R _ { a } { A _ { p } } ^ { 2 } } - \frac { R _ { e , i n } { A _ { p } } ^ { 2 } } { R _ { a } \tau ^ { 2 } } [ ( \frac { R _ { m } } { { A _ { p } } ^ { 2 } } - R _ { a } ) ^ { 2 } + ( \frac { \omega M - K / \omega } { { A _ { p } } ^ { 2 } } - X _ { a } ) ^ { 2 } ]\tag{17}
$$

where $( \omega M { - } K / \omega ) / A _ { p } ^ { ~ 2 } { - } X _ { \mathrm { a } } { = } 0 , \eta _ { a l t }$ achieves a maximum value if all the other variables are constant. Under these conditions, the optimal moving mass $M _ { o p t }$ and the Eq. (17) can be correspondingly rewritten as

$$
M _ { o p t } = K / \omega ^ { 2 } + X _ { a } { A _ { p } } ^ { 2 } / \omega\tag{18}
$$

$$
\eta _ { a l t , o p t } { = } 1 { - } \frac { R _ { m } } { { A _ { p } } ^ { 2 } R _ { a } } { - } \frac { R _ { e , i n } { A _ { p } } ^ { 2 } } { R _ { a } \tau ^ { 2 } } ( \frac { R _ { m } } { { A _ { p } } ^ { 2 } } { - } R _ { a } ) ^ { 2 }\tag{19}
$$

In the optimization design, the acoustic impedance at the inlet of the alternator must be equal to the optimal load acoustic impedance, i.e. $( 7 + 1 1 i ) \times 1 0 ^ { 6 } \mathrm { P a } { \cdot } \mathrm { s } / \mathrm { m } ^ { 3 }$ , which is obtained in Section 4.2. For a double-sided alternator, the inlet acoustic impedance at one side of the alternator, $Z _ { a , s } ,$ is twice that of the total acoustic impedance. The value of $Z _ { a , s } ,$ , therefore, is taken as $( 1 . 4 \substack { + 2 . 2 i } ) \times 1 0 ^ { 7 } \ : \mathrm { P a \cdot s / m ^ { 3 } }$ . ## 5.1 Simulation model

Based on the optimized dimensions from Section 4, the working characteristics of the thermoacoustic trigeneration system are investigated in detail by using the Sage program, which has been used for thermoacoustic refrigerators [34,35], thermoacoustic electric generators [36,37], pulse tube coolers [38,39] and Stirling devices [40,41].The program consists of many model instances such as regenerators, heat exchangers, pistons, tubes and so on, each of which is a collection of component building blocks with parameter input values. The program solves the equations for conservation of mass, momentum, and energy for each model instance. The governing equations of continuity, momentum, and energy equations for the gas domain in the model are [42]

$$
\frac { \partial \rho A } { \partial t } + \frac { \hat { \sigma } \rho u A } { \hat { \sigma } x } = 0\tag{22}
$$

$$
\frac { \partial \rho u A } { \partial t } + \frac { \partial u \rho u A } { \partial x } + \frac { \partial P _ { 1 } } { \partial x } A - F A = 0\tag{23}
$$

$$
\frac { \partial \rho e A } { \partial t } + P _ { 1 } \frac { \partial A } { \partial t } + \frac { \partial } { \partial x } ( u \rho e A + u P _ { 1 } A + q ) - Q _ { w } = 0\tag{24}
$$

where $P _ { 1 } , u , A$ and $\rho$ are the pressure wave, mean-flow velocity, cross section area and gas density, respectively. The variable e is mass-specific total gas energy with $e { = } \kappa { + } u ^ { 2 } / 2$ (κ is mass-specific internal gas energy). The factor F represents the viscous pressure gradient, $Q _ { \mathrm { f } }$ is the heat flow per unit length due to heat transfer, and $q$ is the conductive heat flow in the axial direction. Terms $F ,$ Q<sub>f</sub> and q are formulated as [42]

$$
F = - ( f _ { D } / d _ { h } + K _ { l o s } / l ) \rho u | u | / 2\tag{25}
$$

$$
Q _ { f } = N u ( k / d _ { h } ) S _ { x } ( T _ { w } - T )\tag{26}
$$

$$
q = - N _ { k } k _ { g } \frac { \partial T } { \partial x } A\tag{27}
$$

where $f _ { D } ,$ $d _ { h }$ and $K _ { l o s }$ are the Darcy friction factor, hydraulic diameter and total local loss coefficient respectively. Nu<sub>,</sub> $k _ { g } , \ S _ { x }$ and $( T _ { w } – T )$ are the Nusselt number, gas conductivity, wetted perimeter, and temperature difference between the negative z surface and section-average, respectively. $N _ { k }$ is an axial-conductivity enhancement ratio that is represented as a ratio of the effective gas conductivity to the molecular conductivity. Previous experimental validation of the models in the program showed reasonable accuracy against experimental data. For example, in a cascade two-stage thermoacoustic cooler by Xu et al. Likewise, deviations from the model efficiencies in Guo et al ’s work on a Stirling refrigerator were within 10% [43]. Nevertheless, the work described above show that the simulation results are within engineering accuracy.

## 5.2 Efficiency, cost savings, and emission reductions of the system

A summary of the efficiency, energy cost savings, and emission reductions of the thermoacoustic trigeneration system for waste heat and LNG cold energy recovery is shown in Table 5, using the optimal dimensions defined in section 4. We note that the economic assessment here is limited. A comprehensive analysis of an energy system would require evaluation of annualized investment costs, including installation, operation, and maintenance costs, to show the payback time and the levelized cost of energy for a hypothetical thermoacoustic CCHP system relatively to a conventional system. ## 5.5 Parameter sensitivity control

In practice, it is inevitable for the operating parameters to deviate from the design, which can upset the desired energy balance in the system. These deviations can be explained by combining Eqs. (10), (11) and (13) to obtain the following two equations,

$$
R _ { a } { A _ { p } } ^ { 2 } = R _ { m } + \frac { ( R _ { e , o u t } + R _ { e , i n } ) \tau ^ { 2 } } { [ ( R _ { e , o u t } + R _ { e , i n } ) ^ { 2 } + X _ { e } ^ { ~ 2 } ] }\tag{28}
$$

$$
X _ { \mathrm { a } } { A _ { p } } ^ { 2 } \mathrm { = } \omega M - \frac { K } { \omega } \mathrm { - } \frac { X _ { e } \tau ^ { 2 } } { [ ( R _ { e , o u t } + R _ { e , i n } ) ^ { 2 } + X _ { e } ^ { ' 2 } ] }\tag{29}
$$

The multiplication of Eq. (28) by $| \nu | ^ { 2 } / 2$ (where v is a complex expression of piston velocity) shows the energy balance of the alternator: the term $R _ { a } | A _ { p } \nu | ^ { 2 } / 2$ on the left represents the input acoustic power into the alternator, the first term on the right, $R _ { \mathrm { m } } | \nu | ^ { 2 } / 2$ , denotes the dissipated acoustic power by the mechanical resistance of the piston, and second term on the right characterizes the acoustic power transduced into electric power. This means that, if the parameters deviate from the designed value, the frequency and piston displacement change and achieve a new balance, which may lead to problems if the piston displacement unexpectedly exceeds the designed value. However, for most existing heat-driven thermoacoustic systems, it is difficult to actively modulate the thermal parameters within very short time due to significant thermal response hysteresis in the heat exchangers and regenerators. A feasible solution is to adjust the external electric resistance to keep the displacement of the piston constant at the allowable value [46].

In a practical system, the waste heat and LNG temperatures may fluctuate. We therefore firstly explore how the piston displacement changes according to different temperatures, and then discuss how to keep the piston displacement constant at the allowable value by varying the external electric resistance to avoid it exceeding the limitation. Figure 13(a) shows the influence of the LNG and waste heat temperatures on the piston displacement $S _ { p }$ . The simulation results demonstrate that the larger waste heat temperatures and smaller LNG temperatures call for a larger piston displacement. By actively adjusting the external electric resistance, the piston displacement could be kept constant at a typical allowable value of 8 mm which depends on the size of the piston, as shown in Figure 13(b). The result show that a system with a larger waste heat temperature or smaller LNG temperature, which leads to a larger displacement, can be accommodated by using a smaller value of external electric resistance to limit the piston displacement. In other words, the required external electric resistance should be increased at a smaller waste heat temperature or larger LNG temperature. For example, at the largest waste heat temperature and the smallest LNG temperature, the required external electric resistance for the constant displacement is 52 Ω; while at the smallest waste heat temperature and the largest LNG temperature, the required external electric resistance is increased to 94 Ω.

## 7. Comparisons to similar technologies and future developments 7.1 Comparison with current thermoacoustic systems

Accepted for publication in Energy, 08-Mar-2021

The proposed strategy presents the first concept of jointly combining a thermoacoustic multi-generation system with the utilization of waste heat and cold energy. When compared to current waste-heat-recovery thermoacoustic systems, the proposed system enables much larger power amplification for the same heating temperatures or a much smaller heating temperature required for the same power amplification factor, due to the much larger temperature gradient in the regenerator. For example, one current thermoacoustic system achieves the regenerator power amplification factor of 1.32 at a heating temperature of 400 K [24], while the proposed system achieves the factor of 2.13 at the same heating temperature and a cooling temperature of 110 K. Furthermore, the novel concept of multiple power generation for combined cooling, heating and electricity is attractive for numerous applications.

At present, no similar thermoacoustic CCHP system exists for comparison in terms of system efficiency. However, the proposed advanced configuration enables high efficiency through the introduction of the following concepts. A looped multi-stage configuration is introduced to create a traveling-wave acoustic field leading to high efficiency. The efficiency of a looped multi-stage thermoacoustic cryocooler was shown to be increased by a factor of 1.5 relatively to single-stage system [47]. An efficiency gains of 28% also resulted from the elimination of the phase-shifter tube between the engine and the heat pump unit according to our recent simulations.

## 7.2 Comparison with exsiting systems for waste heat and LNG cold recovery

Most active thermodynamic cycles are based on Rankine or combined cycles, which are usually designed to using waste heat and recover cold energy to provide up to megawatts of power. Nevertheless, there are also sufficiently abundant distributed waste heat and cold energy sources in a much smaller scale. The proposed thermoacoustic system aims to harness such small-scale waste energy. Table 7 presents comparison results of the thermoacoustic system with current existing systems in terms of overall exergy efficiency. The results show that the proposed thermoacoustic system has comparable exergy to current large-scale systems. For example, Sun et al. showed the existing organic-Rankine-cycle based system achieved an exergy efficiency of 21.3% when using LNG cold energy and waste heat at 473 K [48]. Emadi et al. showed that an organic-Rankine-cycle based multiple-generation system achieved an exergy efficiency of 24.9%, by utilizing a geothermal heat source at 450 K and LNG cold energy [49]. Although some other existing systems show higher efficiencies than the thermoacoustic system [50, 51], the efficiency of the proposed system can be further improved by replacing the resonance tubes with mechanical pistons to reduce power losses. In addition to the comparable efficiency, the thermoacoustic system also has the following benefits:

• a simple configuration by integrating multiple energy conversions into a single system,

• adaptability for use as a cogeneration or trigeneration system in various applications,

• no use of ozone-depleting or greenhouse gases,

• high reliability due to the lack of moving parts and low material requirements.

## 7.3 Future developments

Thermoacoustic technology offers great potential for utilizing small-scale waste energy in the future. The current analysis was done for systems of the order of kilowatts. However, this can be considered as a modular unit, which can be scaled up to hundreds of kilowatts without loss of performance, through incorporating different stages in the loop or enlarging system dimensions. One possible application is to utilize waste heat and LNG cold energy in LNG-fueled ship vessels. As shown in Figure 18, the primary power for the vessel is generated by burning natural gas to produce high-temperature combustion gases with sufficient energy to power the gas turbine. The thermoacoustic trigeneration system adopts the exhaust gas of the gas turbine as a heat source, and uses the cold energy of LNG as cold sources. Based on a similar working principle, the proposed system can also possibly be applied in distributed small-scale power plants to simultaneously recover low-temperature waste heat from power plants and utilise LNG cold energy from the LNG user terminal.

![[mu_6912a831_11245aca1fc4801707ac.jpg]]  
Figure 18. A case example of using the thermoacoustic trigeneration system for waste energy recovery in an LNG-fueled vessel.

While thermoacoustic technology shows promising prospects for future applications, it is still in early stages of development, and the demonstrators reported are not yet commercially viable. Currently, three disadvantages must be resolved in order for this technology to be successfully implemented in a variety of real-life applications. The first are the high investment costs, including relatively expensive component materials (e.g., large amounts of copper used in heat exchangers), high manufacturing costs (e.g., wire electrical discharge machining for gas channels) and initial operating charges (e.g., used for high-pressure helium), especially since most components are not available in the market at scale. The second challenge is to ensure a sufficient fatigue life, corresponding to at least several years of operation. The third difficulty is to ensure high efficiency, although the results in this work show that the efficiency of the proposed system is comparable to some existing systems. Resolving these issues will help create a renewable energy conversion technology that has great potential for waste energy unitizations and will present a promising alternative to conventional technologies.

## 8. Conclusions

This paper introduces a first-ever thermoacoustic combined heating, cooling, and electricity system for simultaneously recovering waste heat and LNG cold energy. The proposed system integrates multiple energy conversion processes into one system and allows for small-scale waste energy utilization. Theoretical analyses have been carried out to investigate the characteristics of the system. The following conclusions can be drawn from the results:

(1) The optimized trigeneration system can achieve a combined cooling of 2.19 kW at 283 K, a heating power of 3.55 kW at 323 K and an electric power of 2.27 kW, with an overall exergy efficiency of 24.1 %, where LNG and waste heat temperatures are of 130 K and 500 K, respectively. By considering the economic and environmental aspects, this system allows for around 78.4 MWh of primary fuel energy consumption reduction, and can avoid around 30.6 tons of $\mathrm { C O } _ { 2 }$ emissions per year. These findings suggest that thermoacoustic technology has a significant potential to lower emissions and primary energy consumption.

(2) Load acoustic impedance is introduced to decouple the alternator and thermoacoustic unit for design optimization. By adjusting the load acoustic impedance, an optimum acoustic field can be achieved in the thermoacoustic unit in terms of the highest efficiency. When the optimal impedance for the inlet of the alternator is achieved, the maximum alternator efficiency is dependent on the piston diameter.

(3) The working parameters may deviate from the design values, which may lead to excessive piston displacement. Adjusting the external electric resistance is an effective way of avoiding this problem with a very short response. For instantaneous deviations in the operating temperatures, smaller electric resistance is required to limit larger displacement below the design value.

(4) Thermoacoustic technology can be used as a cogeneration or trigeneration system based on different energy supplies. The results indicate similar energetic, economic, and environmental potential across the different models. Such adaptability allows for the proposed system to be used in various applications.

(5) Compared to current single-source driven thermoacoustic engines, the proposed combined-source engine achieves a much higher power amplification factor with useful efficiencies. Compared to other Rankine cycles for a similar purpose, the proposed system shows a comparable exergy efficiency with additional advantages of simple configuration, expected high reliability, and environmental friendliness.

This work suggests that thermoacoustic multi-generation systems may lead to opportunities for decentralized, small-scale waste energy recovery in e.g., LNG-fueled ship vessels or distributed power plants. Further efforts in system commercialization, cost reduction and efficiency enhancement are required to make thermoacoustic technology economically competitive with other mature energy alternatives.
